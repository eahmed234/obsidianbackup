# ʿĀm al-Fīl (عام الفيل)
*The Year of the Elephant — Allāh Defends His House Before Its Builder Is Even Born*

> [!NOTE] Why It Is Told Here
> Al-Ṣāliḥī places this account among the signs preceding the Prophet's ﷺ birth, stating plainly:
> *"and this destruction came the very year of his birth ﷺ, according to the correct view held by
> most scholars — and their destruction was a mark of honour for him ﷺ and for his city"*
> (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:214).

## 1. Why Abrahah Marched on Makkah
**Dhū Nuwās**, the Jewish king of Ḥimyar, had massacred some twenty thousand Christians of Najrān.
One survivor, **Daws Dhū Thaʿlabān**, fled to Byzantine Caesar, who directed him to the Christian
**Negus of Abyssinia** as the nearer power. The Negus sent two commanders, **Aryāṭ** and
**Abrahah b. al-Ṣabbāḥ**, who conquered Yemen from Ḥimyar — Dhū Nuwās drowning himself in the sea.
The two commanders then fell out; rather than lose their armies to each other, they settled it by
**single combat**. Aryāṭ struck Abrahah across the face, but Abrahah's own page killed Aryāṭ from
behind, and Abrahah — wounded but alive — took sole command of Abyssinian Yemen
(*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:215).

The Negus, angered at the killing of his commander, swore to trample Abrahah's own land and shave his
head in humiliation. Abrahah placated him by sending gifts, a sack of Yemeni soil, and a lock of his
own shorn hair, asking the Negus to tread the soil and so fulfil his oath without further conflict —
and the Negus relented (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:215).

## 2. Al-Qullays, and Its Desecration
Abrahah then built a magnificent church in Ṣanʿāʾ, **al-Qullays**, writing to the Negus: *"I will not
rest until I have turned the pilgrimage of the Arabs to it."* Word of this reached the Arabs, and —
by different reports — a man of Banū Fuqaym defiled it; **Nufayl b. Ḥabīb al-Khathʿamī** fouled its
prayer-direction with excrement and carcasses; or, per Muqātil, young men of Quraysh burned it down
outright in a strong wind (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:216).

## 3. The March on Makkah
Enraged, Abrahah vowed to destroy the Kaʿbah itself and marched with his army and elephant. At
**al-Ṭāʾif**, Thaqīf came out to him and — protecting their own shrine to **al-Lāt** — insisted his
true target lay at Makkah, and provided a guide, **Abū Righāl**, who died en route; Arabs would
afterward stone his grave at al-Mughammas. Abrahah's cavalry, under **al-Aswad b. Maqṣūd**, then
seized livestock from Tihāmah, including **two hundred camels belonging to [[ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib]]**,
then chief of Quraysh (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:217).

## 4. "I Am the Lord of the Camels"
Abrahah sent word through **Ḥunāṭah al-Ḥimyarī** that he had come only to destroy the House, not to
make war on its people, and summoned Makkah's chief to him. **ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib** came, and — one
report adds — as he approached, an elephant that never prostrated to Abrahah **prostrated before him**;
Abrahah's own diviners explained that it had bowed *"to the light between his eyes"* rather than to
Abrahah (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:218).

Abrahah, struck by his bearing, seated him at his own side and asked his request. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib
answered: **his two hundred camels.** Abrahah said, astonished: *"I thought well of you when I saw
you — then I thought little of you when you spoke to me of two hundred camels I seized from you, and
said nothing of a House that is your religion and your fathers' religion, which I have come to
destroy!"* ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib's reply became proverbial:

> «أَنَا رَبُّ الْإِبِلِ، وَإِنَّ لِلْبَيْتِ رَبًّا سَيَمْنَعُهُ»
> *"I am the lord of the camels — and the House has a Lord who will defend it."*
> (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:218)

Abrahah returned the camels. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib went back to Quraysh, told them what had passed, and
ordered them into the hills and ravines for safety. He then took hold of the ring of the Kaʿbah's door
with a company of Quraysh and prayed:

> «لَاهُمَّ إِنَّ الْمَرْءَ يَمْ … نَعُ رَحْلَهُ فَامْنَعْ حِلَالَكْ
> لَا يَغْلِبَنَّ صَلِيبُهُمْ … وَمِحَالُهُمْ عَدْوًا مِحَالَكْ»
> *"O Allāh, a man protects his own baggage — so protect Your own dwelling! Let not their cross and
> their strength ever overcome Your strength!"* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:219)

By Muqātil's account, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib himself did not flee to the hills but remained, saying *"I will
not leave until Allāh decrees His decree,"* watching from high ground with **Abū Masʿūd al-Thaqafī**.

## 5. The Elephant That Would Not Advance
When Abrahah readied his forces to enter Makkah, the elephant — named **Maḥmūd** — knelt and pressed
its underside to the ground the moment it was turned toward the city, though it rose readily when
turned toward Yemen, Syria, or the east. In one report, when the men swore oaths to return it home
afterward, it would flick its ears as if acknowledging the pledge, then rise — a cycle repeated
several times over (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:220).

## 6. The Birds of Ababīl
As night fell, the army sensed impending disaster — the stars themselves, Ibn Isḥāq's account says,
seemed to hang close and nearly speak. At daybreak, Allāh sent **flocks of birds from the sea**, like
swallows, **each carrying three stones** — one in its beak, two gripped in its claws, the size of a
lentil or a chickpea. They lined up over the army's heads and let the stones fall:

> «فَمَا مِنْ حَجَرٍ وَقَعَ عَلَىٰ جَنْبِ رَجُلٍ إِلَّا خَرَجَ مِنَ الْجَنْبِ الْآخَرِ»
> *"Not a stone struck a man's side but it passed clean through to the other."*
> (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:220)

One report, from Ibn ʿAbbās ﵄, describes the stones as black clay-covered pebbles that caused an
outbreak of pustules — the book notes this is said to be **the first appearance of smallpox
(al-jadarī)** among the Arabs, so that any man who scratched his skin found his own flesh falling away
(*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:220–221). Eyewitness descriptions of the stones are preserved from
**Nawfal b. Muʿāwiyah al-Daylī** and **Ḥakīm b. Ḥizām** ﵁ — red, mottled, like banded agate, sized
between a chickpea and a lentil (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:221).

## 7. Abrahah's Death
The army fled in every direction, dying at every watering-place along the way. Abrahah himself was
struck in his own body, and as they carried him back toward Ṣanʿāʾ his fingers **fell away one by
one**, each followed by pus and blood, until he arrived reduced to the size of a fledgling bird —
**and did not die until his heart itself burst** (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:222).

## 8. What ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib Saw
Watching from the heights with Abū Masʿūd, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib described the birds as they passed
overhead — white, the size of swallows, gravel in their beaks like the pellets of a sling, coming in
flights each led by a red-beaked, black-headed, long-necked bird, wheeling to a halt directly over the
army. He sent one of his own sons ahead on a fast horse to see what had befallen them; the boy returned
raising his robe from his thigh in the customary Arab sign of good news, calling out: *"hulikū
jamīʿan"* — **"they have all perished."** ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib said of him: *"My son is the finest
horseman of the Arabs — he would not have bared his leg except as a bearer of glad news, or of
warning"* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:222).

## 9. Precedent: Tubbaʿ's Own Warning
Al-Ṣāliḥī appends an earlier episode Ibn Isḥāq preserves: the Yemeni king **Tubbaʿ**, once urged by
men of Hudhayl to plunder the treasure they claimed lay hidden in the Kaʿbah, set out intending its
destruction — until Allāh sent a wind that twisted his limbs and racked his body. His own Jewish
advisers told him this was a punishment for his intention against the House; he repented in his heart,
was healed at once, and instead entered Makkah to circle the Kaʿbah, clothe it, and feed the people for
six days (*Subul al-Hudā*, 1:223) — a precedent for the same divine protection later shown against
Abrahah.

## 10. The Qurʾānic Seal
This event is the occasion of **Sūrat al-Fīl** (105):

> ﴿أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ﴾
> *"Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the Companions of the Elephant?"* (105:1)
