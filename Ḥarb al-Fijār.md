# Ḥarb al-Fijār (حرب الفجار)
*The Sacrilegious War — The Pre-Islamic Conflict That Violated the Sacred Months and Sacred Sanctuary*

## 1. Historical Background & Definition
*Ḥarb al-Fijār* (The Sacrilegious War) derives its name from *mufājarah*—a mutual descent into transgression and impiety (*fujūr*)—because the warring confederations violated two immutable Arabian sanctities: they shed blood during the **Sacred Months** (*al-Ashhur al-Ḥurum*) and engaged in warfare within the boundaries of the **Sacred Sanctuary** (*al-Ḥaram*) of Makkah (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152–153).

Classical historians, including al-Masʿūdī, catalog four successive Fijār conflicts in pre-Islamic history:
1. *Fijār al-Rajul (al-Ḥirbaḍ)*
2. *Fijār al-Qurḍūb*
3. *Fijār al-Marʾah*
4. **Fijār al-Barrāḍ** (The Fourth and Greatest Fijār, fought between Quraysh/Kinānah and Qays ʿAylān/Hawāzin, witnessed by the Prophet ﷺ) (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:153).

The war erupted when the Messenger of Allāh ﷺ was fourteen or fifteen years of age according to Ibn Hishām, or twenty years of age according to Ibn Isḥāq, roughly twenty years prior to the Prophetic Mission (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

## 2. The Inciting Treachery: Al-Barrāḍ and ʿUrwah al-Raḥḥāl
The conflict was provoked by betrayal along the royal trade routes:
- **Al-Nuʿmān b. al-Mundhir**, the Lakhmid king of al-Ḥīrah, annually dispatched a lucrative royal caravan (*laṭīmah*) laden with perfumes, silks, and precious goods from Iraq to the seasonal trade fair of **ʿUkāẓ** in the Ḥijāz.
- The king sought an influential Arabian noble to guarantee safe passage through tribal territories. **ʿUrwah al-Raḥḥāl b. ʿUtbah** of Hawāzin stepped forward, boasting: *"I grant it safe conduct across all of Najd and Tihāmah."*
- **Al-Barrāḍ b. Qays**, an exiled, reckless renegade of Banū Ḍamrah (a sub-clan of Kinānah), challenged him: *"Do you grant it safe passage even against Kinānah?"* ʿUrwah haughtily replied: *"Yes, against Kinānah and against all creation!"* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).
- Enraged by this slight, al-Barrāḍ stalked ʿUrwah's caravan until it reached **Taymin Dhī Ṭalāl** in the high plateau (*al-ʿĀliyah*). Seizing a moment when ʿUrwah was unguarded and unarmed, al-Barrāḍ ambushed and assassinated him—**in the midst of the Sacred Month**—and plundered the royal caravan (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

## 3. Escalation & The Six Renowned Days of Battle
A dispatch rider galloped to the fair of ʿUkāẓ, warning Quraysh in secret: *"Al-Barrāḍ has slain ʿUrwah!"*
- Fearing immediate blood retribution from the superior numbers of Hawāzin assembled at the fair, the clans of Quraysh and Kinānah packed their encampments and fled toward the boundary of the Sacred Sanctuary (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).
- When Hawāzin discovered the murder, their cavalry gave furious chase, overtaking Quraysh at Nakhlah just outside the boundary stones of the Ḥaram. Fierce hand-to-hand fighting raged until nightfall intervened, allowing Quraysh to slip behind the sanctuary borders where Hawāzin halted out of reverence for the sacred territory (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

The confrontation resumed over the ensuing seasons across **six major recorded battle days** (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152):
1. **Yawm Shamẓah**: Fought near ʿUkāẓ.
2. **Yawm al-ʿAblāʾ**: Also fought near ʿUkāẓ.
3. **Yawm al-Sharb**: The fiercest and most destructive engagement of the entire war. 
   - On this day, the sons of Umayyah b. ʿAbd Shams—**Ḥarb**, **Abū Ḥarb**, **Sufyān**, and **Abū Sufyān**—bound themselves with leather fetters to the ground so none of them could retreat, earning them the enduring tribal title **al-ʿAnābis** ("The Fierce Lions", singular *ʿAnbas*) (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152–153).
4. **Yawm al-Ḥarīrah**: Fought at Nakhlah, where Quraysh suffered heavy losses and were driven back, except for Banū Naṣr who held their positions firm (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

## 4. The Role of the Prophet ﷺ
The Messenger of Allāh ﷺ attended some of these battle days in his youth, accompanying his paternal uncles (led by [[al-Zubayr b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib]] and [[Abū Ṭālib]]) (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

Classical authorities document two distinct roles:
1. **Gathering & Repelling Arrows**: In the early engagements, he retrieved enemy arrows that fell near his uncles and handed them back to supply their quivers (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).
2. **Archery**: Ibn Saʿd transmits that the Prophet ﷺ stated in his own words:
   > «قَدْ حَضَرْتُهُ- يَعْنِي: حَرْبَ الْفِجَارِ- مَعَ عُمُومَتِي، وَرَمَيْتُ فِيهِ بِأَسْهُمٍ، وَمَا أُحِبُّ أَنِّي لَمْ أَكُنْ فَعَلْتُهُ، وَكُنْتُ أَنْبُلُ عَلَى أَعْمَامِي»
   > *"I was present at it—meaning the War of al-Fijār—with my uncles, and I shot arrows in it, and I do not wish that I had not done so, and I supplied arrows to my uncles."* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152; Ibn Saʿd, 1:126–128).
While al-Suhaylī held that the Prophet ﷺ did not actively engage in hand-to-hand combat, al-Ṣāliḥī affirms this authentic transmission attesting to his physical presence and defense of his clan (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:153).

## 5. The Cessation of Arms: The Courage of ʿUtbah b. Rabīʿah
When the opposing confederacies assembled for a decisive reckoning at ʿUkāẓ the following year, **Ḥarb b. Umayyah** (supreme commander of Quraysh and Kinānah) sought to protect his orphaned nephew and ward, the young **[[ʿUtbah b. Rabīʿah]]**, ordering him to stay behind (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

Defying orders, ʿUtbah rode out on his camel and positioned himself directly between the two massed battle lines, calling out in a booming voice:
> «يَا مَعْشَرَ مُضَرَ، عَلَامَ تَفَانَوْنَ؟»
> *"O assembly of Muḍar! Upon what ground are you annihilating one another?"* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

Hawāzin cried back: *"What do you propose?"* ʿUtbah answered:
> *"Reconciliation! We shall count the dead on both sides; whichever faction has suffered surplus casualties, the other shall pay full blood-money (*diyah*), and you shall pardon the blood shed against you!"*
Hawāzin demanded: *"Who will stand as surety for this covenant?"* ʿUtbah proudly answered:
> «أَنَا: عُتْبَةُ بْنُ رَبِيعَةَ بْنِ عَبْدِ شَمْسٍ!»
> *"I will: ʿUtbah b. Rabīʿah b. ʿAbd Shams!"* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

Both sides accepted ʿUtbah's mediation. Quraysh and Kinānah delivered **forty noble hostages** to Hawāzin to guarantee payment of the surplus blood indemnities. Among these forty hostages was the young **[[Ḥakīm b. Ḥizām]]** ﵁ (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152). 

Upon seeing the noble sons in their custody, the chieftains of Banū ʿĀmir b. Ṣaʿṣaʿah were moved to chivalry, pardoned the remaining blood debts, released all forty hostages unharmed, and the war was brought to an end (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

Al-Ṣāliḥī records the famous Arabian historical adage regarding this peace:
> «لَمْ يَسُدْ مِنْ قُرَيْشٍ مُمْلِقٌ- يَعْنِي فَقِيرًا- غَيْرَ عُتْبَةَ وَأَبِي طَالِبٍ، فَإِنَّهُمَا سَادَا بِغَيْرِ مَالٍ»
> *"No man among Quraysh ever attained paramount chieftaincy while impoverished (*mumliq*) except two: ʿUtbah [b. Rabīʿah] and [[Abū Ṭālib]]; for both rose to supremacy through sheer nobility and wisdom without wealth."* (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:152).

The bitter lessons of the Fijār War—the horror of lawlessness and the breakdown of sacred covenants—directly motivated the noble clans to assemble shortly thereafter in the house of Ibn Judʿān to ratify **[[Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl]]** (*Subul al-Hudā*, 2:154).
