# Ṣulḥ al-Ḥudaybiyah (صلح الحديبية)
*The truce made at al-Ḥudaybiyah in Dhū al-Qaʿdah of the year 6*

The **Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyah** was the truce agreed in Dhū al-Qaʿdah of the sixth year after the Hijrah between the Prophet ﷺ and [[Quraysh]], at [[al-Ḥudaybiyah]] near Makkah.[^s5.69][^s5.70] The Prophet ﷺ had set out from Medina with about fourteen hundred Companions to perform the *ʿumrah*,[^s5.41] bringing sacrificial camels and no weapons beyond sheathed swords, after a dream in which he saw himself entering Makkah in safety.[^s5.33][^s5.34] Quraysh refused to let him reach the Kaʿbah. While envoys went back and forth, a false report that [[ʿUthmān b. ʿAffān]] had been killed in Makkah led the Companions to pledge themselves to the Prophet ﷺ beneath a tree, the pledge the Qurʾān praises as the [[Bayʿat al-Riḍwān|Pledge of Riḍwān]].[^s5.48] Quraysh then sent [[Suhayl b. ʿAmr]] to make peace: war was to be laid aside for ten years, and the Muslims were to go home that year and return the next.[^s5.52] Many of the Companions were dismayed by the terms, but on the way home [[Sūrat al-Fatḥ]] was revealed, calling the truce *"a manifest victory"*.[^s5.59] [[Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq|Abū Bakr]] later said that there had been no greater victory in Islam.[^s5.64]

## The vision and the call to the ʿumrah

The cause of the expedition, as the reports give it, was a dream: the Prophet ﷺ was shown that he and his Companions entered Makkah in safety, some with their heads shaved and some with their hair cut short, and that he entered the House, took its key, and stood at ʿArafah with the pilgrims.[^s5.33] He called on the Arabs and the Bedouin around Medina to come out with him, fearing that Quraysh would make war on him or bar him from the House, but many of the Bedouin held back.[^s5.33] Late in Shawwāl, [[Bisr b. Sufyān al-Khuzāʿī|Busr b. Sufyān al-Khuzāʿī]] came to Medina as a Muslim. The Prophet ﷺ asked him to stay and go with them, *"for, if Allāh wills, we are going on the ʿumrah"*; Busr bought sacrificial camels for him and kept them at Dhū al-Jadr, a pasture six miles from Medina, until the departure, when they were handed to Nājiyah b. Jundub al-Aslamī, who drove them on to Dhū al-Ḥulayfah.[^s5.33][^s5.80]

Al-Wāqidī and Ibn Saʿd say that the Prophet ﷺ left [[ʿAbdullāh b. Umm Maktūm|Ibn Umm Maktūm]] in charge of Medina; Ibn Hishām names [[Numaylah b. ʿAbdillāh al-Laythī]]; al-Balādhurī adds Abū Ruhm Kulthūm b. al-Ḥuṣayn, and says some held that all of them were appointed, with Ibn Umm Maktūm leading the prayer.[^s5.33]

## The march from Medina

The Prophet ﷺ bathed, put on two garments woven in Ṣuḥār, and rode out on his camel al-Qaṣwāʾ. He took [[Umm Salamah]] with him, and with her Umm ʿUmārah, Umm Manīʿ Asmāʾ bint ʿAmr and Umm ʿĀmir al-Ashhaliyyah. The Muhājirūn and the Anṣār went with him, with the Arabs who had joined them, none of them doubting that they would enter Makkah because of the vision. They carried no arms but swords in their sheaths.[^s5.33][^s5.34]

He left on a Monday at the new moon of Dhū al-Qaʿdah and prayed the noon prayer at [[Dhū al-Ḥulayfah]]. There the sacrificial camels, seventy in number, were covered, and he marked some of them on the right side of the hump while they faced the qiblah; Nājiyah b. Jundub marked the rest and hung a sandal from each one's neck, and the Muslims marked and garlanded their own camels too. They had two hundred horses with them. The Prophet ﷺ sent Busr b. Sufyān ahead as a scout and put [[ʿAbbād b. Bishr]] in front with twenty horsemen; it is also said that Saʿd b. Zayd al-Ashhalī commanded them.[^s5.34] He prayed two *rakʿahs* and, as his camel rose facing the qiblah at the mosque of Dhū al-Ḥulayfah, entered the state of *iḥrām* for the *ʿumrah*, so that people would feel safe from war and know that he had come only to visit the House and honour it. Most of his Companions entered *iḥrām* with him, though some waited until al-Juḥfah.[^s5.34]

On the road he passed the Bedouin of Banū Bakr, Muzaynah and Juhaynah and called on them to come out. They made excuses about their property and told one another that Muḥammad wanted to lead them against a people fully armed, and that he and his Companions were so few that one slaughtered camel would feed them all; they would never come back from this journey.[^s5.34] A party of Banū Nahd refused his invitation to Islam and offered him milk, which he declined, saying, *"I do not accept the gift of an idolater"*; the Muslims bought it from them instead.[^s5.34] One of the sacrificial camels in Nājiyah's charge broke down, and the Prophet ﷺ told him to slaughter it, dip its garlands in its blood and leave it for the people, eating none of it himself.[^s5.34]

Several rulings for pilgrims in *iḥrām* came out of the journey. [[Abū Qatādah al-Anṣārī|Abū Qatādah]], who was not in *iḥrām*, killed a wild ass, and the Prophet ﷺ told the Companions in *iḥrām* who had eaten of it that it was lawful for them since none of them had helped him hunt it.[^s5.34][^s5.35] When al-Ṣaʿb b. Jaththāmah gave him a wild ass at al-Abwāʾ or Waddān, he returned it, explaining that he did so only because they were in *iḥrām*.[^s5.35] Kaʿb b. ʿUjrah was so troubled by lice that they fell on his face; the Prophet ﷺ told him to shave his head, and the verse on the ransom of fasting, charity or a sacrifice (al-Baqarah 2:196) was revealed. He was told to fast three days, feed six poor people, or sacrifice an animal.[^s5.35][^s5.36] At al-Juḥfah the Prophet ﷺ addressed the people beneath a tree, telling them that he would go before them, and that he had left them what would keep them from going astray if they held to it: the Book of Allāh and the Sunnah of His Prophet.[^s5.36]

## Quraysh prepare to stop him

When Quraysh heard that the Prophet ﷺ was coming, they were alarmed. They asked whether he meant to come in among them with his army on the pretext of an *ʿumrah*, so that the Arabs would hear that he had entered Makkah by force despite the war between them, and they swore that it would never happen.[^s5.36] They sent [[Khālid b. al-Walīd]] ahead with two hundred horsemen to Kurāʿ al-Ghamīm, a spot in a valley between Rābigh and al-Juḥfah, and called out the Aḥābīsh and those who obeyed them; Thaqīf joined them. They camped at Baldaḥ with their women and children and posted ten lookouts on the hills to pass word of his movements back to them.[^s5.36][^s5.81] (One report, from al-Kharāʾiṭī, adds that a voice from Abū Qubays had roused Makkah against him the night he set out.)[^s5.36b]

Busr b. Sufyān came back from Makkah and met the Prophet ﷺ at Ghadīr al-Ashṭāṭ beyond ʿUsfān with the news: Quraysh had come out with their milch camels and their young, had put on leopard skins, and were camped at Dhū Ṭuwā swearing that he would never enter; and Khālid's horsemen were at Kurāʿ al-Ghamīm.[^s5.37] The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Alas for Quraysh! War has eaten them up. What would it cost them to leave me to the rest of the Arabs?"* If the Arabs overcame him, Quraysh would have what they wanted; if Allāh gave him the victory, they could enter Islam with their numbers intact, or fight while they still had strength. He would go on striving for what Allāh had sent him with until Allāh made it prevail, or until his neck was severed.[^s5.37][^s5.82]

He then asked the Muslims for their advice: should they fall on the families of the tribes who had come to help Quraysh, or make for the House and fight whoever barred them from it? Abū Bakr said that they had come for the *ʿumrah*, not to fight anyone, and should go on, fighting only whoever turned them back; [[Usayd b. Ḥuḍayr]] agreed.[^s5.37] [[al-Miqdād b. ʿAmr|Al-Miqdād b. al-Aswad]] told him that they would not say to him what the Children of Israel said to their prophet — *"Go, you and your Lord, and fight; we shall sit here"* — but *"Go, you and your Lord, and fight; we shall fight with you."*[^s5.37] *"March, then, in the name of Allāh,"* the Prophet ﷺ said.[^s5.38]

Khālid came close enough to see the Muslims and drew up his horsemen between them and the qiblah; the Prophet ﷺ sent ʿAbbād b. Bishr with his horsemen to face him. At the noon prayer the Prophet ﷺ led the Muslims in prayer while Khālid watched. Khālid said that they had been off their guard and could have been attacked, but that another prayer was coming that was dearer to them than themselves and their children. Between the noon and afternoon prayers [[Jibrīl]] brought the verse of the prayer in danger (al-Nisāʾ 4:102), and the Prophet ﷺ led the afternoon prayer in that form.[^s5.38]

## The road through Dhāt al-Ḥanẓal

That evening the Prophet ﷺ told the army to bear right, because Khālid was at al-Ghamīm with Quraysh's horsemen and he did not want to meet him. [[Buraydah b. al-Ḥuṣayb]] al-Aslamī offered to guide them to the pass of Dhāt al-Ḥanẓal. Khālid did not notice them until he saw the dust of the army, and then rode off to warn Quraysh.[^s5.38] Buraydah led them down a rough track among ravines until he lost his way, though he said he had travelled it many times; Ḥamzah b. ʿAmr al-Aslamī took over and was lost in turn in the thick trees, and ʿAmr b. ʿAbd Nuhm al-Aslamī finally brought them to the head of the pass.[^s5.38][^s5.39] ʿAmr said the pass had been as narrow as a sandal strap, but it opened wide before him, so that the army walked down it side by side, talking, on a night as bright as if the moon were full.[^s5.39]

Jābir reports that the Prophet ﷺ said that whoever climbed the pass of al-Murār would have his sins taken from him as they were taken from the Children of Israel, and the first to climb it were horsemen of al-Khazraj.[^s5.39] When they came out onto level ground he told them to say, *"We ask Allāh's forgiveness and turn to Him"*; they said it, and he told them that this was the *ḥiṭṭah* the Children of Israel had been offered and had refused to say. The next morning he said that the whole company had been forgiven except one man on a red camel. The man, of Banū Ḍamrah, was found with [[Saʿīd b. Zayd]], but when he was urged to go to the Prophet ﷺ to be prayed for, he said that finding his lost camel mattered more to him. He went looking for it, slipped in the hills of Sarāwiʿ and fell to his death, and wild animals ate him.[^s5.39][^s5.40]

## The halt at al-Ḥudaybiyah

As the Prophet ﷺ came near al-Ḥudaybiyah, al-Qaṣwāʾ knelt on the slope leading down to the enemy and would not get up, and the people said that she had become stubborn. He answered:[^s5.40]

> «ما خلأت القصواء وما ذاك لها بعادة … ولكن حبسها حابس الفيل عن مكة»
> *"Al-Qaṣwāʾ has not become stubborn, and it is not her habit; she has been held back by the One who held back the Elephant from Makkah."*[^s5.40]

He then swore that whatever plan Quraysh asked of him that day which honoured what Allāh had made sacred, he would grant it to them.[^s5.40] Al-Ṣāliḥī explains the comparison with the Elephant: had the Companions forced their way into Makkah and been fought, blood would have been shed as it would have been had the Elephant entered; but Allāh knew that many of the Meccans would enter Islam, and there were believing men, women and children in the city who might have been killed unawares.[^s5.71][^s5.72] The camel rose when he urged her on, and he turned aside and camped at the far end of al-Ḥudaybiyah, by a shallow water hole with little water in it.[^s5.40]

### The water

The people soon drained the water hole and complained to the Prophet ﷺ of thirst. He drew an arrow from his quiver and had it planted in the water, which then rose until everyone had drunk their fill; al-Miswar says they were scooping water with their vessels while sitting on the edge of the well.[^s5.40] Nājiyah b. al-Aʿjam of Aslam, who went down with the arrow according to al-Wāqidī, said that the Prophet ﷺ first rinsed his mouth into a bucket of the well's water and told him to pour it into the well and stir it with the arrow, and the water boiled up until he could barely climb out. The idolaters had already taken the waters of Baldaḥ, and this was the only well.[^s5.41] Al-Barāʾ b. ʿĀzib's account has the Prophet ﷺ sitting at the edge of the well, performing ablution into a bucket, rinsing his mouth, praying and pouring it in, until it gave enough for them and their mounts.[^s5.41] Al-Ṣāliḥī takes the two accounts together, as al-Wāqidī's report from Aws b. Khawlī and Abū al-Aswad's from ʿUrwah both mention the ablution and the arrow.[^s5.73] Who went down into the well is disputed: Nājiyah b. Jundub or Nājiyah b. al-Aʿjam, [[al-Barāʾ b. ʿĀzib]], or Khālid b. ʿUbādah al-Ghifārī, each of whom is named in a report; al-Ṣāliḥī suggests they may have worked together.[^s5.40][^s5.73]

[[ʿAbdullāh b. Ubayy b. Salūl|ʿAbdullāh b. Ubayy]] was at the water. When Aws b. Khawlī rebuked him, he said he had seen the like before; but when the Prophet ﷺ asked him, he admitted he had never seen anything like it, and asked him to pray for his forgiveness. His son ʿAbdullāh asked the same, and the Prophet ﷺ did so.[^s5.41] Jābir and Salamah b. al-Akwaʿ describe another occasion at al-Ḥudaybiyah when the people had no water left except what was in the Prophet's small leather vessel. He put his hand into it and water began to stream from between his fingers like springs, so that they all drank and made their ablutions; asked how many they were, Jābir said: *"Had we been a hundred thousand it would have been enough for us. We were fifteen hundred."*[^s5.42] Ibn Ḥibbān held that this happened on a different occasion from the well.[^s5.73]

During those days it rained one night. In the morning the Prophet ﷺ told the people that Allāh had said: *"This morning some of My servants believe in Me and some disbelieve. Whoever says, 'We were given rain by the grace and mercy of Allāh,' believes in Me and disbelieves in the stars; whoever says, 'We were given rain by such-and-such a star,' believes in the stars and disbelieves in Me."* Ibn Ubayy had said that it was the autumn rain of the star al-Shiʿrā.[^s5.42] ʿAmr b. Sālim and Busr b. Sufyān of Khuzāʿah brought sheep and camels as gifts, which the Prophet ﷺ divided among his Companions.[^s5.42]

## The envoys of Quraysh

### Budayl b. Warqāʾ

When the Prophet ﷺ had settled at al-Ḥudaybiyah, [[Budayl b. Warqāʾ al-Khuzāʿī|Budayl b. Warqāʾ]], who later became a Muslim, came to him with men of [[Banū Khuzāʿah|Khuzāʿah]], among them ʿAmr b. Sālim and [[Khirāsh b. Umayyah]]. Khuzāʿah, some of them Muslims and some at peace with him, were his trusted confidants in Tihāmah and kept nothing from him.[^s5.43] Budayl reported that Kaʿb b. Luʾayy and ʿĀmir b. Luʾayy — that is, all of Quraysh in Makkah — had called out the Aḥābīsh and were camped at the waters of al-Ḥudaybiyah with their women and children, swearing not to let him reach the House.[^s5.43][^s5.73] The Prophet ﷺ replied:

> «إنّا لم نأت لقتال أحد، إنما جئنا لنطوف بهذا البيت، فمن صدّنا عنه قاتلناه»
> *"We have not come to fight anyone. We have come only to go round this House, and we will fight whoever bars us from it."*[^s5.43]

War had worn Quraysh down, he said; if they wished, he would grant them a truce for a period, during which they would leave him to deal with the rest of the people. If the people overcame him, that was what Quraysh wanted; if he prevailed, they could join the others in Islam or fight when they had recovered their strength. But if they refused, he would go on striving until his neck was severed.[^s5.43]

Budayl took this to Quraysh. Some of them did not want to hear it, and [[ʿIkrimah b. Abī Jahl]] and al-Ḥakam b. al-ʿĀṣ said that he would never enter while one of them was left alive; but [[ʿUrwah b. Masʿūd al-Thaqafī|ʿUrwah b. Masʿūd]] advised them to listen, and [[Ṣafwān b. Umayyah]] and al-Ḥārith b. Hishām asked for the report. Budayl told them that the Prophet ﷺ had come for the *ʿumrah*, not to fight.[^s5.43]

### ʿUrwah b. Masʿūd

ʿUrwah reminded Quraysh that he was one of them through his mother, Subayʿah bint ʿAbd Shams, and that he had come to their aid with his family and followers when the people of ʿUkāẓ failed them. He told them that Budayl had brought them a sensible plan and offered to go to the Prophet ﷺ himself.[^s5.43][^s5.74] He told the Prophet ﷺ that if it came to war he would either destroy his own people — and no one had ever heard of a man who destroyed his own people and kin — or be abandoned by the rabble of strangers he saw around him. Abū Bakr, sitting behind the Prophet ﷺ, answered him with an insult to al-Lāt and asked whether he thought they would run away and abandon him. ʿUrwah said that if he had not owed Abū Bakr a favour he would have answered him: Abū Bakr had once given him ten camels towards a blood-money he had to pay.[^s5.44]

As he talked, ʿUrwah kept reaching for the Prophet's beard. [[al-Mughīrah b. Shuʿbah]], standing over the Prophet ﷺ with a sword and wearing a helmet, struck his hand each time with the end of the scabbard and told him to keep his hand away, since it was not fitting for an idolater to touch him. When ʿUrwah asked who the man was, the Prophet ﷺ smiled and told him it was his brother's son, al-Mughīrah; ʿUrwah called him treacherous and reminded him of an old act of treachery at ʿUkāẓ whose consequences ʿUrwah had only lately washed away.[^s5.44] Al-Ṣāliḥī notes that it was the Arabs' habit to take hold of the beard of the man they were speaking to, usually between equals; the Prophet ﷺ overlooked it to win ʿUrwah over, while al-Mughīrah stopped it out of reverence for him.[^s5.74]

ʿUrwah watched the Companions closely. Whenever the Prophet ﷺ spat, a man caught it in his hand and rubbed it on his face and skin; when he gave an order they rushed to carry it out; when he made his ablution they almost fought over the water; they took any hair that fell from him, lowered their voices when he spoke, and did not stare at him out of respect.[^s5.44] Returning to Quraysh, ʿUrwah told them:

> «يا قوم إني وفدت إلى الملوك: كسرى وقيصر والنجاشي وإني والله ما رأيت ملكا قط أطوع فيما بين ظهرانيه من محمد في أصحابه»
> *"My people, I have been sent to kings — to Chosroes, to Caesar and to the Negus — and by Allāh, I have never seen a king obeyed among his people as Muḥammad is among his Companions."*[^s5.44][^s5.45]

He urged them to accept the plan, warning that these men would give their lives if Quraysh chose the sword. Quraysh told him not to speak like that; they would turn the Prophet ﷺ back that year and he could come again the next. ʿUrwah left with his followers for al-Ṭāʾif.[^s5.45]

### Al-Ḥulays b. ʿAlqamah and Mikraz b. Ḥafṣ

Next came al-Ḥulays b. ʿAlqamah al-Kinānī, a chief of the Aḥābīsh; al-Ṣāliḥī says he knows of no report that he became a Muslim. Seeing him approach, the Prophet ﷺ said that he was of a people who revered sacrificial animals, and told them to drive the animals towards him. When al-Ḥulays saw them streaming down the side of the valley in their garlands, the hair on their necks eaten away from their long confinement, and the people in *iḥrām*, unkempt after half a month of waiting, coming out to meet him with the *talbiyah*, he cried out that such people should not be kept from the House: Lakhm, Judhām, Kindah and Ḥimyar could go on pilgrimage, and the son of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib was to be turned away?[^s5.45] Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī and Ibn Saʿd say that he did not reach the Prophet ﷺ, out of awe at what he saw. He went back and told Quraysh that this was not what they had allied with him for, and that they would either let the Prophet ﷺ through or he would take the Aḥābīsh away to a man. They told him to sit down, since he was only a Bedouin who knew nothing.[^s5.45]

Then Mikraz b. Ḥafṣ asked to go. Seeing him, the Prophet ﷺ said, *"This is a treacherous man,"* and spoke to him as he had to Budayl and ʿUrwah.[^s5.46] Al-Ṣāliḥī, following al-Wāqidī, explains the description: Mikraz had killed ʿĀmir b. Yazīd, the chief of Banū Bakr, by stealth after a settlement between the two tribes, and was known for treachery; he also led the night raid on the camp described below.[^s5.74]

## Khirāsh, ʿUthmān and the skirmishes

The Prophet ﷺ sent Khirāsh b. Umayyah to Quraysh on one of his own camels, called al-Thaʿlab, to tell their leaders why he had come. ʿIkrimah b. Abī Jahl hamstrung the camel and they meant to kill Khirāsh, but the Aḥābīsh protected him and let him go, and he returned.[^s5.46]

The Prophet ﷺ then wanted to send [[ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb|ʿUmar]], but ʿUmar said that Quraysh knew his hostility to them and none of his clan, Banū ʿAdī, was left in Makkah to protect him. He proposed ʿUthmān, who had more kinsmen and standing there.[^s5.46] The Prophet ﷺ sent ʿUthmān to tell Quraysh that they had come only for the *ʿumrah* and to invite them to Islam, and to visit the believing men and women in Makkah and tell them that victory was near and that Allāh would soon make His religion so manifest there that no one would have to hide his faith.[^s5.46] ʿUthmān met Quraysh at Baldaḥ and gave his message; they told him that the Prophet ﷺ would never enter by force.[^s5.46] Abān b. Saʿīd, who later became a Muslim, took him under his protection, set him on his own horse and rode behind him into Makkah. ʿUthmān went to the leaders of Quraysh one by one, and to the oppressed believers, who rejoiced and sent their greetings to the Prophet ﷺ.[^s5.47] Quraysh offered to let him go round the House, but he refused to do so before the Prophet ﷺ had. He stayed three days in Makkah.[^s5.47]

At al-Ḥudaybiyah the Muslims supposed that ʿUthmān had gone round the House, but the Prophet ﷺ said he did not think ʿUthmān would do so while they were kept from it. When ʿUthmān came back and the Muslims said he must have had his fill of the House, he told them that they thought badly of him: he would not have done it had he stayed a year, and he had refused when Quraysh invited him.[^s5.47]

The camp was guarded at night in turns by Aws b. Khawlī, ʿAbbād b. Bishr and [[Muḥammad b. Maslamah]].[^s5.47] On one of the nights that ʿUthmān was in Makkah, Quraysh sent fifty men under Mikraz b. Ḥafṣ to circle the camp and catch a Muslim off guard. Muḥammad b. Maslamah captured them, though Mikraz escaped.[^s5.47][^s5.48] Ten Muslims had meanwhile gone into Makkah with the Prophet's permission — Kurz b. Jābir al-Fihrī, [[ʿAbdullāh b. Suhayl|ʿAbdullāh b. Suhayl b. ʿAmr]], ʿAbdullāh b. Ḥudhāfah al-Sahmī, Abū al-Rūm b. ʿUmayr al-ʿAbdarī, [[ʿAyyāsh b. Abī Rabīʿah]], [[Hishām b. al-ʿĀṣ|Hishām b. al-ʿĀṣ b. Wāʾil]], Abū Ḥāṭib b. ʿAmr, ʿUmayr b. Wahb al-Jumaḥī, [[Ḥāṭib b. Abī Baltaʿah]] and ʿAbdullāh b. Abī Umayyah — under ʿUthmān's protection, or, it is said, in secret; they were found and seized.[^s5.48] Hearing that their men were held, a band of Quraysh came out and the two sides exchanged arrows and stones. The Muslims captured twelve horsemen, and one Muslim, Ibn Zunaym, was shot dead by the idolaters as he looked out over the pass.[^s5.48]

Quraysh sent Suhayl b. ʿAmr, Ḥuwayṭib b. ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā and Mikraz to the Prophet ﷺ. Suhayl said that the detention of his men and the fighting had not been the decision of the wise among Quraysh, but the work of their fools, and asked for the release of their prisoners. The Prophet ﷺ said he would not release them until his own men were sent back, and Suhayl agreed that this was fair. ʿUthmān and the ten were sent back and the Qurashī prisoners released.[^s5.48] But before ʿUthmān and his companions arrived, word reached the Prophet ﷺ that they had been killed.[^s5.48]

## The Pledge under the Tree

When he heard that ʿUthmān had been killed, the Prophet ﷺ called the people to pledge themselves to him, saying: *"We will not leave until we have fought it out with these people."*[^s5.48] He went to the camp of Banū Māzin b. al-Najjār and sat beneath a green tree. *"Allāh has commanded me to take the pledge,"* he said, and the people crowded to him so thickly that all the baggage of Banū Māzin was trampled. They took up their arms, of which they had few; Umm ʿUmārah took hold of a tent pole and tied a knife at her waist.[^s5.48] [[Salamah b. al-Akwaʿ]] recalls that they were resting at midday when a crier called out that the Holy Spirit had come down and summoned them to the pledge, and they came to the Prophet ﷺ under an acacia (*samurah*) tree.[^s5.48][^s5.49] One report from Jābir calls the tree a lote tree (*sidrah*).[^s5.67]

Salamah pledged three times: first among the first, again in the middle when the Prophet ﷺ asked him, and again at the end. Seeing him unarmed, the Prophet ﷺ gave him a shield, and when he later asked where it was, Salamah said he had given it to his uncle ʿĀmir, who had none; the Prophet ﷺ laughed.[^s5.49] [[ʿAbdullāh b. ʿUmar]] pledged before his father: ʿUmar had sent him to fetch a horse from one of the Anṣār, and he pledged on the way and then brought ʿUmar the news, and ʿUmar went and pledged. This, says Nāfiʿ, is why people said that Ibn ʿUmar became a Muslim before ʿUmar.[^s5.49] Ibn ʿUmar described the Prophet ﷺ as wearing a cotton shirt, a padded robe and a cloak, with a sword, while al-Nuʿmān b. Muqarrin held the branches of the tree away from his head.[^s5.49] Jābir says that ʿUmar held the Prophet's hand while they pledged, and that all pledged except al-Jadd b. Qays al-Anṣārī, who hid behind his camel.[^s5.49][^s5.50]

The first to pledge is given in one report as Abū Sinān al-Asadī, who said: *"I pledge myself to what is in your heart"* — to strike with his sword before the Prophet ﷺ until Allāh gave him victory or he was killed — and the people pledged on the same terms.[^s5.50] Salamah's own report, transmitted by Muslim, makes him the first; al-Ṣāliḥī holds that the two can be reconciled.[^s5.74][^s5.75] According to Ibn Ḥajar, al-Wāqidī gave this place to [[Abū Sinān b. Miḥṣan|Abū Sinān's]] son [[Sinān b. Abī Sinān al-Asadī|Sinān]].[^i3.157]

Salamah said they pledged to die; Jābir said they pledged not to flee, and not to die. Ibn Ḥajar sees no contradiction: to pledge to the death meant not to flee even at the cost of their lives, and one narrator described the pledge itself, the other where it might lead.[^s5.49][^s5.50][^s5.75] For ʿUthmān, who was absent, the Prophet ﷺ struck one of his hands on the other and said it was for ʿUthmān, and the Prophet's hand for ʿUthmān was better than their own hands for themselves.[^s5.50]

The Prophet ﷺ told them, *"You are the best people on earth,"* and said that none who pledged beneath the tree would enter the Fire.[^s5.51] The next year they could not find the tree again. Years later Ibn ʿUmar said that its being hidden had been a mercy from Allāh, and when ʿUmar heard that people were going to pray at it, he threatened them and had it cut down.[^s5.50] Al-Ṣāliḥī explains that ʿUmar feared the ignorant would come to venerate it.[^s5.76] Suhayl and his companions, seeing how quickly the Muslims pledged and readied themselves for war, became afraid and hurried towards a settlement; then news came that the report of ʿUthmān's death was false.[^s5.51] The pledge is the subject of al-Fatḥ 48:10 and 48:18.[^s5.66][^s5.67]

## The terms of the truce

When Suhayl, Ḥuwayṭib and Mikraz reported back, the men of judgement among Quraysh agreed that it was best to make peace on condition that the Prophet ﷺ go home that year without reaching the House, so that the Arabs who had heard of his march would hear that Quraysh had turned him back; he could come back the next year, stay three days and sacrifice his animals. They sent Suhayl to make the treaty, telling him that the Arabs must never be able to say that the Prophet ﷺ had entered by force.[^s5.51][^s5.52] When the Prophet ﷺ saw Suhayl coming, he said that Quraysh wanted peace if they were sending this man.[^s5.52]

The Prophet ﷺ sat cross-legged, with ʿAbbād b. Bishr and Salamah b. Aslam standing over him in mail and helmets. Suhayl knelt and the two spoke at length, their voices rising and falling, until ʿAbbād told Suhayl to lower his voice before the Prophet ﷺ.[^s5.52] They agreed on these terms:[^s5.52]

- War was to be laid aside between the two sides for ten years, and people were to be safe from one another.[^s5.52]
- The Prophet ﷺ was to go back that year. The next year Quraysh would leave Makkah to him for three days, and he would enter with no weapons but a rider's arms, the swords kept in their sheaths.[^s5.52]
- Anyone of Quraysh who came to Muḥammad without his guardian's permission was to be returned to his guardian, even if he followed Muḥammad's religion; anyone of Muḥammad's followers who came to Quraysh would not be returned.[^s5.52]
- Past causes of hostility were to be put away, and there was to be no stealing or treachery between them.[^s5.90][^s5.91]
- Any tribe that wished could enter into alliance with Muḥammad, and any that wished could enter into alliance with Quraysh.[^s5.52]

At once Khuzāʿah declared themselves in alliance with Muḥammad, and Banū Bakr with Quraysh.[^s5.52]

The duration is given as ten years by Ibn Isḥāq, Ibn Saʿd and most authorities, and al-Ḥākim reports it from ʿAlī. Reports from Ibn ʿĀʾidh and Ibn ʿUqbah give two years, which al-Ṣāliḥī takes as the time the truce actually lasted before Quraysh broke it; a report of four years he calls weak and contrary to the sound reports.[^s5.76]

### ʿUmar's objection

The Muslims disliked the terms, but Suhayl would accept nothing else. Before the document was written, ʿUmar went to the Prophet ﷺ and asked: was he not truly the Prophet of Allāh? Were they not in the right and their enemies in the wrong? Were not their dead in Paradise and the enemy's in the Fire?[^s5.52]

> «علام نعطي الدّنيّة في ديننا؟»
> *"Then why should we accept humiliation in our religion?"*[^s5.52]

The Prophet ﷺ answered: *"I am the servant of Allāh and His Messenger. I will not disobey Him, and He will not abandon me; He is my helper."* ʿUmar asked whether he had not told them they would go to the House and circle it; the Prophet ﷺ asked whether he had said it would be that year, and told him that he would come to it and circle it.[^s5.52] ʿUmar went angrily to Abū Bakr and asked him the same questions, and Abū Bakr gave him the same answers, telling him to hold fast to the Prophet's stirrup until he died, for he was in the right.[^s5.52][^s5.53] Al-Ṣāliḥī takes Abū Bakr's agreement with the Prophet's answer as a sign that he was the most perfect of the Companions and the one who best understood him.[^s5.78]

ʿUmar is reported as saying that he had never doubted since he became a Muslim except on that day, and that [[Abū ʿUbaydah b. al-Jarrāḥ]] told him to seek refuge with Allāh from Satan and mistrust his own opinion.[^s5.53] ʿUmar said that he kept on giving charity, fasting, praying and freeing slaves because of what he had said that day, until he hoped it would turn out for good.[^s5.53] Ibn Ḥajar, quoted by al-Ṣāliḥī, explains these as good works done to atone for his hesitation in obeying at first, not as further questioning; and al-Ṣāliḥī himself considers that ʿUmar held back only to understand the wisdom in the matter, and was excused, even rewarded, for an honest judgement.[^s5.78][^s5.79]

### Writing the document

Suhayl asked for the terms to be written down, and the Prophet ﷺ called [[ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib|ʿAlī]] to write.[^s5.53] When the Prophet ﷺ told him to write *"In the name of Allāh, the Most Merciful, the Compassionate,"* Suhayl objected that he did not know who al-Raḥmān was, and asked for *"In Your name, O Allāh,"* as they used to write. The Muslims insisted on the full formula, but the Prophet ﷺ told ʿAlī to write *"In Your name, O Allāh."*[^s5.53] When he dictated *"This is what Muḥammad, the Messenger of Allāh, has agreed,"* Suhayl said that if they believed he was the Messenger of Allāh they would not have barred him from the House or fought him, and asked for *"Muḥammad b. ʿAbdullāh."*[^s5.53][^s5.54] ʿAlī refused to erase the words. Al-Wāqidī adds that Usayd b. Ḥuḍayr and [[Saʿd b. ʿUbādah]] held ʿAlī's hand and said that it would be the sword between them unless he wrote *"Muḥammad, the Messenger of Allāh."* The Prophet ﷺ quieted them, asked ʿAlī to show him the place, and erased it with his own hand. He told ʿAlī that the same would one day be asked of him and that he would have to give it while under pressure.[^s5.54] Al-Ṣāliḥī counts this as a prophecy fulfilled at the Arbitration, and regards ʿAlī's refusal as an act of reverence, not disobedience.[^s5.77]

Al-Zuhrī explains the Prophet's concessions by his oath at the halt to grant any plan that honoured what Allāh had made sacred.[^s5.54] Al-Nawawī, quoted by al-Ṣāliḥī, adds that the concessions did no harm: *"In Your name, O Allāh"* means the same as the *basmalah*, and *"Muḥammad b. ʿAbdullāh"* does not deny that he is the Messenger; harm would only have come had they asked for something unlawful, such as honouring their gods.[^s5.78]

The Prophet ﷺ asked that they be allowed to go round the House that year; Suhayl refused, so that the Arabs would not say Quraysh had been forced, and it was written for the following year. When Suhayl added that anyone who came from Quraysh to the Prophet ﷺ, even of his religion, must be sent back, the Muslims exclaimed in protest. The Prophet ﷺ told them that whoever went from them to the idolaters, Allāh had sent him far away, and that for whoever came from the idolaters to them, Allāh would make relief and a way out.[^s5.54]

According to al-Barāʾ, ʿAlī was the scribe. A report from Suhayl's son ʿAmr says that the copy his family kept was written by Muḥammad b. Maslamah; al-Ṣāliḥī reconciles the two by taking ʿAlī's as the original and Muḥammad b. Maslamah's as a copy made for Suhayl.[^s5.76] The treaty was witnessed by Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, [[ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAwf]], ʿAbdullāh b. Suhayl b. ʿAmr, [[Saʿd b. Abī Waqqāṣ]], Maḥmūd b. Maslamah and ʿAlī, and on the other side by Mikraz b. Ḥafṣ.[^s5.56]

### The raiders who were pardoned

During the negotiations armed men of Quraysh tried more than once to take the Muslims by surprise. ʿAbdullāh b. Mughaffal describes thirty armed young men who rushed at them; the Prophet ﷺ prayed against them, they were seized, and he let them go when they admitted that they had no pledge of protection from anyone. Anas speaks of eighty armed men coming down from the direction of al-Tanʿīm, who were taken and pardoned.[^s5.54][^s5.55] Salamah b. al-Akwaʿ was resting under a tree after the truce, when Muslims and Meccans had begun to mix, and moved away from four Meccans who were speaking ill of the Prophet ﷺ. When a cry went up that Ibn Zunaym had been killed, he took their weapons while they lay asleep and drove them to the Prophet ﷺ, and his uncle ʿĀmir brought in a man named Mikraz; the Prophet ﷺ pardoned them, saying that the first act of treachery and its repetition should be theirs.[^s5.55] The verse *"It is He who held back their hands from you and your hands from them in the valley of Makkah, after He had given you victory over them"* (al-Fatḥ 48:24) was revealed about these men.[^s5.54][^s5.55][^s5.80]

## Abū Jandal

While the treaty was being settled, [[Abū Jandal b. Suhayl|Abū Jandal]], Suhayl's son, came shuffling in his fetters from the lower part of Makkah and threw himself among the Muslims. His father had chained and imprisoned him; he had escaped, kept off the road and come through the hills to al-Ḥudaybiyah.[^s5.55] The Muslims welcomed him, but Suhayl struck him in the face with a thorny branch, seized him by the collar, and said that this was the first case in which he held the Prophet ﷺ to the agreement. The Prophet ﷺ said that the document had not yet been completed; Suhayl answered that in that case he would never make peace on any terms. The Prophet ﷺ asked him to grant Abū Jandal to him, and Suhayl refused. Mikraz and Ḥuwayṭib said that they granted him their protection, took him into a tent, and his father let him be.[^s5.55] Abū Jandal cried out:

> «أي معاشر المسلمين أردّ إلى المشركين وقد جئت مسلما؟»
> *"Muslims! Am I to be sent back to the idolaters when I have come to you as a Muslim?"*[^s5.55]

He had been severely tortured.[^s5.55][^s5.56] The Prophet ﷺ told him to be patient and hope for his reward, for Allāh would make relief and a way out for him and the oppressed believers with him; they had made a treaty with these people and given their word, and they would not break it. ʿUmar walked beside Abū Jandal telling him to be patient, that the blood of an idolater was the blood of a dog, and kept the hilt of his sword close to him, hoping, he said, that Abū Jandal would take it and strike his father; but he spared his father.[^s5.56]

The Companions had set out never doubting that they would enter Makkah, because of the Prophet's vision. When they saw the truce and the return, and what the Prophet ﷺ had taken on himself, they were so distressed that they were close to ruin, and the matter of Abū Jandal added to it.[^s5.56]

Al-Ṣāliḥī reports two explanations from al-Khaṭṭābī of how Abū Jandal could be sent back: he could protect his life by concealing his faith if he had to, and he was being returned to his own father, who would probably not go so far as to kill him.[^s5.77] Whether a truce may lawfully require Muslims who flee to be returned is disputed: some hold that it may, on the evidence of Abū Jandal and Abū Baṣīr; the Ḥanafīs consider it abrogated; the Shāfiʿīs allow it for a Muslim on whom emigration is not obligatory.[^s5.77][^s5.78]

## Sacrifice and shaving

When the document was finished, the Prophet ﷺ told the Companions to get up, sacrifice their animals and shave. No one moved, though he said it three times. Troubled, he went in to Umm Salamah and told her that the Muslims had perished: he had commanded them and they had not obeyed. She told him not to blame them, for they were overwhelmed by what he had taken on himself in making the truce and by going home without victory, and advised him to go out without speaking a word to anyone, sacrifice his camels and call his barber to shave him.[^s5.56] He went out with his garment under his right arm, took the spear, and slaughtered his camels saying *"In the name of Allāh; Allāh is greatest."* The Muslims rushed to their own animals, crowding so close to slaughter them that they almost fell on one another. They shared the sacrifices, one camel for seven men.[^s5.56][^s5.92] Al-Ṣāliḥī notes that Imām al-Ḥaramayn said he knew of no woman whose advice proved so right as Umm Salamah's here, though others have named the daughter of Shuʿayb in the story of Mūsā.[^s5.79]

The Prophet's sacrifice was seventy camels. When the idolaters barred the way the animals' faces were turned back, and Ibn ʿAbbās says they moaned as they moan for their young.[^s5.56][^s5.57] Among them was a fine camel of the Mahrī breed that had belonged to [[Abū Jahl (ʿAmr b. Hishām)|Abū Jahl]], with a silver ring in its nose, which the Prophet ﷺ had brought to anger the idolaters. It broke loose and ran all the way to Abū Jahl's house in Makkah; ʿAmr b. ʿAnamah al-Anṣārī went after it, and Suhayl made the young men of Makkah give it up. It is said they offered several camels for it, but the Prophet ﷺ said that had it not been named as a sacrifice they would have accepted, and sacrificed it.[^s5.57] [[Ṭalḥah b. ʿUbaydillāh|Ṭalḥah]], ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAwf and ʿUthmān also sacrificed camels they had brought. The Prophet ﷺ camped outside the sacred territory but prayed within it, and he sent twenty of his camels with a man of Aslam to be sacrificed for him at al-Marwah.[^s5.57]

He then went into his tent of red leather and was shaved by Khirāsh b. Umayyah, who threw the hair onto a green acacia beside him; the people took it from the tree and shared it among themselves, and Umm ʿUmārah kept some strands, which she would wash and give to the sick to drink. Some shaved and some cut their hair short. The Prophet ﷺ put his head out of the tent and prayed three times for Allāh's mercy on those who had shaved, and then on those who had cut their hair; asked why he had favoured the first, he said it was because they had not doubted.[^s5.57] According to Ibn Saʿd, a strong wind carried their hair into the sacred territory.[^s5.57]

Al-Ṣāliḥī suggests several reasons why the Companions had been slow to obey: they may have thought the command a recommendation, or hoped for a revelation cancelling the truce and allowing them into Makkah that year, or been stunned by the humiliation they felt despite their confidence that they could reach the House by force.[^s5.79]

The Prophet ﷺ stayed at al-Ḥudaybiyah nineteen days, or twenty nights, according to al-Wāqidī and Ibn Saʿd; Ibn ʿĀʾidh says the whole expedition took a month and a half.[^s5.57]

## The return and Sūrat al-Fatḥ

On the way home the army camped at Marr al-Ẓahrān and then at ʿUsfān, and ran out of food. The people asked permission to slaughter some of their riding camels, and the Prophet ﷺ gave it, but ʿUmar urged him instead to collect what provisions were left and pray over them for a blessing. A leather mat was spread and the people brought what they had, a handful or a little more, the most being a *ṣāʿ* of dates. Salamah judged the pile to be about the size of a goat lying down, and they were fourteen hundred. The Prophet ﷺ prayed, and they ate until they were full, filled their vessels, and as much was left over; he laughed and bore witness that he was the Messenger of Allāh.[^s5.58]

One of the Companions said that this was no victory: they had been barred from the House, their sacrifices had been barred, and two believers who had come to them had been sent back. The Prophet ﷺ called it a bad thing to say. It was the greatest of victories, he said: the idolaters were content to push them away gently from their land, asked them for terms and sought safety from them, after seeing from them what they hated, and Allāh had brought them home safe and rewarded. Had they forgotten Uḥud, and the day of the Confederates? The Muslims answered that Allāh and His Messenger had spoken the truth, and that he knew Allāh and these matters better than they did.[^s5.58][^s5.59]

ʿUmar asked the Prophet ﷺ about something three times on the journey and got no answer; afraid that a revelation would come down about him, he rode ahead of the army, until he was called back and told: *"A sūrah has been revealed to me tonight that is dearer to me than the world and all that is in it,"* and the Prophet ﷺ recited *"We have given you a manifest victory, so that Allāh may forgive you your past and future sins"* (48:1–2).[^s5.59] Mujammiʿ b. Jāriyah says that at Kurāʿ al-Ghamīm they saw the people urging their camels on to hear the revelation, and the Prophet ﷺ recited the sūrah to them; when a man asked whether it was really a victory, he said: *"Yes, by Him in whose hand is my soul, it is a victory."*[^s5.59] In Ibn Saʿd's version Jibrīl congratulated him, and then the people did.[^s5.59] Anas reports that when the Companions congratulated the Prophet ﷺ and asked what was in store for them, the verse promising the believers gardens beneath which rivers flow (48:5) was revealed.[^s5.59][^s5.60]

## Abū Baṣīr and the men on the coast

After the Prophet ﷺ reached Medina, [[Abū Baṣīr (ʿUtbah b. Usayd)|Abū Baṣīr]] ʿUtbah b. Asīd b. Jāriyah al-Thaqafī, an ally of Banū Zuhrah, escaped from his people and came to him on foot as a Muslim. Al-Akhnas b. Sharīq and Azhar b. ʿAbd ʿAwf al-Zuhrī wrote demanding his return under the treaty, and sent the letter with Khunays b. Jābir of Banū ʿĀmir b. Luʾayy and a freedman called Kawthar as guide; they arrived three days after him, and [[Ubayy b. Kaʿb]] read the letter to the Prophet ﷺ.[^s5.61] The Prophet ﷺ handed Abū Baṣīr over to them, telling him that they had given their word, that treachery was not right for them in their religion, and that Allāh would make relief and a way out for him and the Muslims with him. Abū Baṣīr protested at being sent back to be tormented for his faith, but went. As he left, Muslims whispered to him that one man can be worth a thousand, and ʿUmar told him that he was a man and had a sword.[^s5.61]

At Dhū al-Ḥulayfah, at the noon prayer, Abū Baṣīr prayed two *rakʿahs* in the mosque and shared his dates with his two guards. The ʿĀmirī drew his sword and boasted that he would strike the Aws and the Khazraj with it all day long; Abū Baṣīr asked to see whether it was sharp, and when he had it in his hand, killed him with it. Ibn ʿUqbah has a different report, that Abū Baṣīr took the sword in his teeth while his guard slept and cut his bonds with it. Kawthar ran in terror all the way to Medina.[^s5.61][^s5.62] The Prophet ﷺ, seeing him come, said that the man had seen something frightening; Kawthar told him that his companion had killed Kawthar's own, and he gave Kawthar his protection. Abū Baṣīr came in with the sword at his side and said that the Prophet ﷺ had kept his obligation, and that he himself had saved his religion from being tested.[^s5.62] The Prophet ﷺ said:

> «ويل أمّه مسعر حرب»
> *"Woe to his mother! What a kindler of war!"*[^s5.62]

— if only, as other wordings add, he had men, or anyone, with him.[^s5.62]

He refused to take a fifth of the dead man's spoils, saying that if he did, Quraysh would think he had not kept to the treaty, and told Abū Baṣīr to keep them and go wherever he liked.[^s5.62] Al-Ṣāliḥī notes that the killing was not treachery, since Abū Baṣīr had not been party to the treaty, and the Prophet ﷺ did not condemn it.[^s5.79]

Understanding from those words that he would be sent back again, Abū Baṣīr left with five Muslims who had come with him from Makkah and made for the sea coast, settling, according to Ibn Shihāb, between al-ʿĪṣ and Dhū al-Marwah in the land of Juhaynah, on the route of Quraysh's caravans. Al-Wāqidī says he had only a handful of dates, which lasted him three days, and then lived on fish thrown up on the shore.[^s5.62] In Makkah, Suhayl was furious at the killing and swore he would not move from the Kaʿbah until the blood-money was paid; [[Abū Sufyān b. Ḥarb]] called it folly, and al-Akhnas said that Banū Zuhrah would not pay for a man they had not killed. No blood-money was paid.[^s5.62]

Word of Abū Baṣīr reached the Muslims held in Makkah, and they slipped away to join him; al-Wāqidī says ʿUmar wrote to them with the Prophet's words about him. Abū Jandal escaped with seventy riders who had become Muslims. They did not want to go to the Prophet ﷺ while the truce held, nor to stay among their own people, and joined Abū Baṣīr; he gave the command to Abū Jandal because he was of Quraysh, and Abū Jandal led them in prayer.[^s5.62][^s5.63] Men of Ghifār, Aslam and Juhaynah gathered to them until they numbered three hundred fighters. They seized every caravan of Quraysh that passed and killed those with it.[^s5.63] Quraysh sent Abū Sufyān to the Prophet ﷺ to beg him to send for them, telling him that whoever came to him from Makkah he could keep. The Prophet ﷺ wrote to Abū Baṣīr and Abū Jandal to come to him, and told the others to go home and leave Quraysh and their caravans alone. The letter reached Abū Baṣīr as he lay dying; he read it and died with it in his hands. Abū Jandal buried him there and built a mosque by his grave, then came to the Prophet ﷺ with some of the men while the rest went home, and Quraysh's caravans were safe after that.[^s5.63] ʿUrwah says that those who had wanted the Prophet ﷺ to keep Abū Jandal from his father then understood that obeying the Prophet ﷺ was better for them.[^s5.63]

## "The greatest victory"

When the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah the following year and shaved his head, he said, *"This is what I promised you."* On the day of the conquest of Makkah, holding the key of the Kaʿbah, he called for ʿUmar and told him the same, and again at ʿArafah during the Farewell Pilgrimage.[^s5.63] Abū Bakr used to say that there had been no greater victory in Islam than the truce of al-Ḥudaybiyah:

> «ما كان فتح في الإسلام أعظم من صلح الحديبية»
> *"There was no victory in Islam greater than the truce of al-Ḥudaybiyah."*[^s5.64]

People had not understood what was between the Prophet ﷺ and his Lord, he said; men are hasty, but Allāh does not hurry for their haste. At the Farewell Pilgrimage he saw Suhayl b. ʿAmr bringing the Prophet's camels to him to be sacrificed, and picking up the hair from his shaven head and pressing it to his eyes; and he remembered how Suhayl had refused to allow *"In the name of Allāh, the Most Merciful, the Compassionate"* to be written, and praised Allāh for guiding him to Islam.[^s5.64]

Al-Zuhrī said that there had been no greater victory before it: until then there had been fighting wherever the two sides met, but once people were safe they talked and argued with one another, and no one of sense was told of Islam without hastening to enter it, so that as many entered Islam in those two years as had entered before, or more.[^s5.64] Ibn Hishām points out that the Prophet ﷺ went to al-Ḥudaybiyah with fourteen hundred men, and two years later to the conquest of Makkah with ten thousand.[^s5.65] Ibn Ḥajar adds that the truce made it possible for those who had been afraid to become Muslims and come to Medina, as [[Khālid b. al-Walīd]] and [[ʿAmr b. al-ʿĀṣ]] did, and that what looked like a humiliation for the Muslims was in reality their strength: Muslims and idolaters mixed freely, the Muslims recited the Qurʾān to the idolaters and argued for Islam openly, and those who had hidden their Islam came into the open.[^s5.64][^s5.65] Al-Balādhurī, quoted by al-Ṣāliḥī, describes how Quraysh, mixing with the Muslims and visiting Medina, heard of the Prophet's teaching and his life in detail, so that many became Muslims before the conquest of Makkah and the rest were ready to do so when it came; and the Bedouin, who had been waiting on Quraysh, followed them.[^s5.80]

## Qurʾānic connection

The Prophet's ﷺ vision, the pledge and the truce are the subject of [[Sūrat al-Fatḥ]]. The scholars, as Ibn Ḥajar summarises them, differ on which "victory" is meant in each verse.[^s5.64] Al-Ṣāliḥī's commentary on the sūrah connects its verses with the events as follows:[^s5.64][^s5.65][^s5.66][^s5.67][^s5.68][^s5.69]

| --- | --- | --- |
| 48:1 | إِنَّا فَتَحْنا لَكَ فَتْحًا مُبِينًا | The truce of al-Ḥudaybiyah, called a victory because the idolaters sought peace after the Muslims had prevailed, and because it led to the conquest of Makkah; Anas says the victory is the truce, al-Barāʾ that it is the Pledge of Riḍwān |
| 48:4 | هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ | Calm and steadiness sent down on the believers' hearts |
| 48:10 | إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُبايِعُونَكَ إِنَّما يُبايِعُونَ اللَّهَ | The Pledge of Riḍwān; "the hand of Allāh" is a figure of speech, Allāh being far above having limbs |
| 48:11–12 | سَيَقُولُ لَكَ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ | The Bedouin around Medina who stayed behind, claiming their property and families kept them, and thinking the Messenger would never return |
| 48:15 | إِذَا انْطَلَقْتُمْ إِلى مَغانِمَ | The spoils of Khaybar, reserved for those present at al-Ḥudaybiyah |
| 48:18 | لَقَدْ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ إِذْ يُبايِعُونَكَ تَحْتَ الشَّجَرَةِ | The Pledge under the tree; the "near victory" is [[Ghazwat Khaybar|Khaybar]] |
| 48:24 | وَهُوَ الَّذِي كَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ | The Meccan raiders captured and pardoned |
| 48:25 | هُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرامِ | Quraysh barring the Muslims and their sacrifices; the believing men and women in Makkah who might have been killed unawares |

Ibn Ḥajar's resolution of the different views is that the victory of 48:1 is al-Ḥudaybiyah, the beginning of the manifest victory; the "near victory" of 48:18 is Khaybar on the sound view, its lands being divided among those who had been at al-Ḥudaybiyah; and the victory of Sūrat al-Naṣr is the conquest of Makkah by agreement of all.[^s5.64][^s5.65]

## The place, the date and the numbers

Al-Ḥudaybiyah takes its name from a well there, and the name came to be used for the whole place. It is about one stage from Makkah and nine from Medina, and al-Muḥibb al-Ṭabarī says that most of it lies within the sacred territory. The *yāʾ* of the name is pronounced light by al-Shāfiʿī and the philologists and doubled by most scholars of hadith; al-Nawawī says both are well known.[^s5.69][^s5.70]

The expedition took place in the year 6, in Dhū al-Qaʿdah according to the majority. Hishām b. ʿUrwah reports from his father that it was in Shawwāl, but in this he stands alone, and Abū al-Aswad's report from ʿUrwah agrees with the majority; ʿĀʾishah and Anas both say that all of the Prophet's *ʿumrahs* were in Dhū al-Qaʿdah.[^s5.70]

The reports disagree on how many went with the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Barāʾ and Jābir, in some reports, give fourteen hundred; other reports from Jābir, and those of Saʿīd b. al-Musayyab and Mujammiʿ b. Jāriyah, give fifteen hundred; ʿAbdullāh b. Abī Awfā gives thirteen hundred, and a report from al-Zuhrī eighteen hundred.[^s5.41][^s5.42][^s5.51][^s5.70] Ibn Ḥajar reconciles fourteen and fifteen hundred by supposing that they were somewhat more than fourteen hundred, which some rounded up and others down, and al-Nawawī relies on this; al-Bayhaqī prefers fourteen hundred. Thirteen hundred may be what Ibn Abī Awfā knew of, or the fighting men alone.[^s5.70] Ibn ʿUqbah gives sixteen hundred, a report from Salamah seventeen hundred, and Ibn Saʿd 1,525.[^s5.71] Ibn Isḥāq's figure of seven hundred, worked out from the number of sacrificial camels, was followed by no one, and Ibn al-Qayyim calls it a plain mistake.[^s5.71]
